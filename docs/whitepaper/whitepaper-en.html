1. Summary

“The most powerful force in Islamic giving is trust. We are not replacing that trust — we are giving it proof.”— Akbar Amirzoda, Founder

Zakat is the third pillar of Islam: one-fortieth of qualifying wealth, owed every lunar year by each Muslim whose wealth exceeds the nisab. The World Bank and IRTI/IsDB estimate the annual Zakat potential — what is owed, not what is collected — at $50–600 billion. Less than $25 billion of it is distributed through formal institutions. The rest is given in cash, through intermediaries, or not at all, and the person who gave it can rarely check whether it reached a family in real need.

AM Network is a verification and transparency service for Zakat and Sadaqah: we check each family in person, publish an open, tamper-evident report of every payout, and never hold the money — the donor gives directly to the family.

What that means in practice

  • In person. A trained AM Network volunteer visits the family and fills a checklist. Whoever brought the family never verifies it.
  • A person decides. The need checklist shows its points openly and is capped at 100. It is a hint; the admin approves help only after an in-person check that recommends it.
  • No custody. The donor gives to the family directly — cash, Alif, Korti Milli or bank transfer. 100% reaches the family. We take no fee from Zakat, and there is no token.
  • Open report. Each payout appears at amnetwork.io/transparency/ without names, phones or addresses. Every entry carries a SHA-256 fingerprint linked to the one before it, so a quiet change breaks the chain, and anyone can check that in the browser.

Stage

Pilot in preparation: Dushanbe, Tajikistan, 10–20 families, around Ramadan 2027 (February–March), using only the founder’s own and his relatives’ Zakat. There is no public fundraising. The software for families, verifiers, donors and the admin is built; before the pilot we will consult a lawyer in Tajikistan, seek review by a Hanafi scholar, train volunteers and run a trial with 2–3 families. We are not raising investment now.

Also available

AM Academy — Sharia-compliant financial literacy, explained for four audiences, with a downloadable certificate — and free Islamic tools in 10 languages: Quran, hadith, dua, prayer times, Qibla, Islamic calendar, 99 Names, tasbeeh and a Zakat calculator.

2. The Problem

2.1 Zakat is large, and mostly invisible

  • Of an estimated $50–600 billion owed each year, less than $25 billion is distributed through formal institutions.
  • Donors have no reliable way to see whether their Zakat reached an eligible family.
  • Many intermediaries publish little or no accounting, and what is published is usually a yearly total that an outsider cannot check.
  • Many of the people most in need are invisible to formal charity: they do not know how to apply, or nobody speaks for them.
“A donor gives his Zakat. Weeks later he still does not know whether the family was really in need, or whether the money arrived.”— The gap AM Network works on.

2.2 Why it is hard to fix

  • Documents are easy to forge. Paper certificates always were; with generative AI, digital ones are too. A remote check of documents misses what a visit sees.
  • Every extra hand costs something. When money passes through intermediaries, there are costs, delays and room for leakage at each step.
  • Reports are not verifiable. A spreadsheet can be edited after the fact and nobody would know.
  • Trust is personal. In practice, Muslims give through people they know. A system that asks them to trust a distant institution instead has to earn it.

AM Network does not try to replace personal trust. It adds two things to it: a careful, recorded check of each family by a person who visited, and a public record of each payout that cannot be quietly changed.

4

5

3.1 What AM Network does not do

  • We do not collect, hold, pool or distribute Zakat. There is no AM Network account that Zakat passes through, and no escrow.
  • We take nothing from Zakat — no fee, no percentage, no deduction.
  • No algorithm approves or rejects a family. A person decides after a visit.
  • We do not publish anything that identifies a family.

3.2 Whose money is in the pilot

Only the founder’s own Zakat and that of his relatives. We do not ask the public for money. That keeps the pilot small and honest: if something goes wrong, it is our own Zakat and our own responsibility, and we can learn before anyone else depends on the process.

4. How a Family Is Checked

4.1 Who checks

Trained AM Network volunteers. Only the admin can give someone the verifier role, and only to people the admin knows personally. Later, staff of registered partner charities may also verify. Mosques and imams are not a fixed part of the process: the system must work without depending on any religious institution.

4.2 The visit

The volunteer visits the family where it lives and records what was confirmed in person:

  • an identity document was seen;
  • the family lives at the address given;
  • the size of the household;
  • income and expenses were clarified;
  • whether the family receives other regular help.

The volunteer then recommends help or do not help, and may add notes that only the admin sees.

4.3 The need checklist

The checklist is shown to the volunteer with its points next to each item, so nobody has to guess how the number is made. The total is capped at 100. It is a transparent hint for the person who decides, not a decision.

Applications are also tagged with situation categories, mapped to the eight categories of Zakat recipients (asnaf) in the Quran (9:60): widows and single mothers; orphans; elderly people forced to work; disabled people; families below the poverty line; students from low-income families; people in riba-based debt; refugees and displaced persons; the chronically ill without care; large families with one breadwinner; food-insecure households; new Muslims in hardship; survivors of trafficking and forced labour; victims of conflict and disasters; and people invisible to traditional charity. Only the category, never the story, appears in the public report.

AI does not decide anything in this process. At most it may later help explain the checklist to a family or a donor.

4.4 Rules the database enforces

These rules are written into the database itself, not only into the web page, so a modified page or a direct request cannot get around them:

  • Whoever brought a family cannot be assigned to verify it.
  • An application can be approved only if at least one in-person check recommends help.
  • A payout can be recorded only for an approved application.
  • A family is marked “help delivered” only by recording a payout, never by hand.
  • Only the admin can change anyone’s role.
  • Every step — submitted, assigned, checked, approved, paid — is written to the application’s history.

5.2 The SHA-256 hash chain

When a payout is recorded, the database builds one line of text from the entry’s facts and the fingerprint of the entry before it:

payload = prev_hash | ref | amount | currency | kind | date | receipt_sha256
entry_hash = sha256(payload)

An illustrative entry (the hashes are shortened): 0000…0000|AMN-2027-0001|1500.00|TJS|zakat|2027-02-20|9f2c…e41aThe opening entry links to a fingerprint of 64 zeros. Each later entry includes the previous entry’s entry_hash as its prev_hash. Change any past amount, date or reference, and that entry’s fingerprint no longer matches — nor does every entry after it. The fingerprints are computed by the database at the moment of recording, and the app has no way to edit or delete a payout: entries can only be added.

5.3 Receipts kept as fingerprints

A receipt or a transfer screenshot is never uploaded. The admin’s browser computes its SHA-256 fingerprint on the device, and only those 64 characters are stored and included in the payload. If a question arises later, showing the original file to an auditor proves it is the same receipt: any change to the file produces a different fingerprint.

5.4 Check it yourself

The “Check the chain” button on the report page re-checks every entry in the visitor’s own browser: that the payload matches the fields shown, that sha256(payload) equals the entry’s fingerprint, and that each entry points to the one before it. The same can be done with any SHA-256 tool, for example printf '%s' "<payload>" | sha256sum.

What the chain proves, and what it does not

It proves that a published entry has not been quietly changed: an edit breaks the chain. It does not prove by itself that the money changed hands — that rests on the in-person check, the receipt fingerprint and the people involved. And whoever runs the database could, in principle, rebuild the whole chain; anyone who saved an earlier copy of the report, or its latest fingerprint, would then see that it no longer matches. We state this plainly rather than call the report “immutable”.

6. Data Protection

Families trust us with sensitive information: income, health, debts, household. We collect only what the check needs, keep it private, and publish none of it.

  • Where it lives. The pilot runs on Supabase (managed PostgreSQL), hosted in the EU region.
  • Row-level security. Every table is locked by default. The database decides, row by row, who may read what — not the web page.
  • Consent. An application cannot be saved without the family’s consent to its data being stored and seen only by the verifier and the admin.
  • No files. Receipts are kept only as fingerprints (Section 5.3). The cabinet uses no analytics and is hidden from search engines.
  • Keys. The key embedded in the page is public by design; it grants nothing that row-level security does not allow. The service key and database password are held by the founder only and never appear in code.

7. Sharia Approach

Our starting point is the Hanafi school, which most Muslims in Tajikistan follow.

  • Zakat is the donor’s act. Zakat is fulfilled when ownership passes to an eligible recipient (tamlik). In our model the donor hands the Zakat to the family directly, so ownership passes from donor to recipient without anyone in between. The intention (niyyah) stays with the donor.
  • We are not an amil. In Hanafi fiqh a private organisation not appointed by the authorities is not a Zakat collector (amil), and so has no claim on the collectors’ share. At most it acts as the donor’s agent. AM Network goes further: it does not handle the money at all, and takes nothing from Zakat.
  • Eligibility is checked, not assumed. The visit confirms, among other things, that the family has no wealth above nisab. The final judgment of eligibility is a person’s, after a visit.
  • Sadaqah is recorded separately from Zakat in the report, because the rules for the two differ.

Status

We are seeking review of this model by a Hanafi scholar before the pilot. AM Network is not certified by any scholar or Sharia board, and we name no one as an advisor until they have agreed.

Our free tools do not issue religious rulings. Quran text, translations and recitations are shown live from Quran.com exactly as published; hadith come from an open dataset with sources; any AI translation is labelled as such.

8. Legal Approach

  • No custody. AM Network never receives, holds or forwards other people’s money. On our reading, this means it does not need a money-transfer licence; it is not a bank, a payment provider or a fund.
  • Confirm before the pilot. We will consult a lawyer in Tajikistan before the pilot to confirm that reading and to check the rules for processing families’ personal data and photos.
  • Partner charities later. When a donor wants to give through an organisation, that will be a registered charity operating under its own licence. No partner is confirmed today.
  • A legal entity for AM Network is not needed for a pilot that handles no money; we will decide on it after the pilot, with legal advice.

9. Sustainability

There is no income in year one, and that is a deliberate choice. The pilot’s costs are small — hosting on free tiers and the founder’s time — and the founder covers them.

Later, and only in ways that never touch Zakat

  • Optional donor tips, shown as a separate line and paid separately — never deducted from Zakat.
  • Grants for transparency and social-impact work.
  • Service fees from partner charities, paid from their own funds, for the verification and reporting we do for them.

Never

  • A fee or percentage taken from Zakat.
  • A token or any speculative instrument.
  • Selling or sharing families’ data; advertising.

We are not raising investment now.

What this system cannot do

  • It cannot make a dishonest person honest. It makes dishonesty harder to hide.
  • It does not prove that money changed hands — people and receipts do. It proves that the record was not quietly changed afterwards.
  • It does not replace the donor’s own responsibility for his Zakat.

What changed since v1.1

Version 1.1 (October 2026) described a different product. In November 2026 the founder chose a simpler model. What was dropped, and why:

  • Blockchain and smart contracts removed. v1.1 planned to move Zakat through escrow smart contracts on the Base blockchain, with Solana as a backup and LayerZero between chains, settled in stablecoins. Holding other people’s Zakat brings licensing, custody, security-audit and Sharia questions that a small pilot does not need. Transparency does not require holding the money: a public, SHA-256-chained report gives tamper-evidence without it. The Solidity code stays in the repository as an archived prototype; it is not used or deployed.
  • The “ujrah 1–2.5%” fee removed. Under Hanafi fiqh a private organisation is the donor’s agent, not an amil, and nothing passes through us to charge on. We take nothing from Zakat. Sustainability is now described in Section 9.
  • No token, NFT or DAO. The planned NFT certificates for AM Academy are dropped; the certificate is a downloadable file.
  • “Oracle network” replaced by trained volunteers. Mosque imams are no longer a fixed part of verification; the process should not depend on any religious institution.
  • “AI need score 0–100” replaced by a transparent checklist with visible points, capped at 100. A person decides after a visit.
  • Dates. “Testnet beta, Q4 2026” replaced by a pilot in Dushanbe around Ramadan 2027.
  • Sharia status. “Designed to comply; fatwa pending” replaced by: seeking a Hanafi scholar’s review before the pilot; not certified.
  • Removed: the seed round and use of funds, revenue projections, the Islamic finance and fintech market sizes, and the hiring list. We are not raising investment, and those figures do not describe a verification pilot.
  • Kept: the Zakat potential as a range ($50–600 billion owed, under $25 billion distributed formally), the 15 situation categories mapped to the eight asnaf, and the honest-status rule: nothing is described as live before it is.
